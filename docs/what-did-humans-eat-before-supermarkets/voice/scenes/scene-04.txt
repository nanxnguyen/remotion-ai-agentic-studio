Now, step away from Nara's fictional journey and look at the archaeological evidence.

At several European sites dating to around thirty thousand years ago, researchers identified starch grains from wild plants on grinding tools.

The findings suggest that people processed plant foods and may have produced flour-like materials long before agriculture became widespread.

This matters because prehistoric diets are sometimes portrayed as almost entirely meat-based.

The evidence tells a more complicated story.

Wild plants could be important food resources, and preparing them required knowledge, tools, and time.

But these findings come from particular sites. They do not tell us that every prehistoric community ate the same plants or prepared food in the same way.
